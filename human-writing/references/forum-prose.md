# Like Someone Talking Seriously Online

This file is for Zhihu answers, forum long posts, WeChat articles, blogs, comments, character pieces, history pieces and industry analysis.

Use the natural speaking relationship of a long online post. A person really knows some things; facing a crowd of strangers willing to listen, they tell the story from beginning to end.

## Have a Speaking Position First

There's always a person in the article. This person doesn't necessarily say "I" frequently, but readers know where they stand.

They might have experienced it personally, might have researched, might know the industry, or might have just seriously read the materials and formed judgment. All four positions work. Can't pass research off as personal experience, can't pass speculation off as insider knowledge.

No personal experience doesn't mean the author disappears. The author can still say which explanation they trust more, which material changed their view, which popular saying seems wrong to them. First-person expressing judgment isn't the same as faking first-person experience.

The most lively part of reality articles is often where knowledge comes from. Fictional posts show how the narrator knows, misunderstands and discovers inside the story, not faking real sources for it.

- What event made the author notice this question.
- How they first understood, which material corrected them.
- Which detail only someone doing this would notice.
- Which chunks of material contradict each other; how did the author finally choose.
- Which judgment is just personal view; author accepts responsibility but doesn't pretend it's conclusion.

Each piece picks one or two that appear naturally. Getting all of them becomes a new template.

## Writing for This Reader in Front of You

Zhihu answers have a question naturally in front. Forum posts have a posting reason naturally in front. Public articles also need to mentally supplement a reader—they just learned what, what will they ask next.

Don't simultaneously lecture "to everyone." Imagine the reader as a smart, interested, unfamiliar-with-background ordinary person. When they understand, move on; when they might misunderstand, add half a sentence.

Speaking to readers isn't the same as frequently calling "everyone," "friends." Real communication happens in explanation order.

## Article Moves Forward Along Local Questions

Long posts can have overall direction without needing to pre-assemble a structure framework. Write one paragraph, ask just one thing: what most wants to know now.

Common natural progressions are:

- First tell what happened, readers ask why.
- First give judgment, readers ask what grounds it.
- First tell someone's situation, readers ask what happened next.
- First explain normal situation, readers ask what's special this time.
- First tell result, readers ask what went wrong in between.

These can alternate repeatedly. Don't line them into a fixed five-step.

Tangents also grow from immediate wording. Tell about someone's loan; casually explain how informal lending worked then; finish explaining and continue the loan story. It's useful. Mention someone's loan and suddenly recall the rise and fall of civilizations—usually just showing off association ability.

## Sentences Deliver the Main Structure First

State what a person did first, then supplement it with why, when, conditions, and examples.

Don't write like this.

> After years of repeated attempts with consistently unstable income sources, what finally pushed him to change direction was an invitation from an old colleague.

Could write like this.

> He struggled for a few years and never made stable money. Later an old colleague reached out, asking if he'd want to team up on a project; that's when he changed direction.

The right side can run very long. The key is readers first see how this person struggled and who came to him, only then lands on direction change. Readers don't have to carry a long chain of modifiers first.

When one sentence has three or four "of," first find the actual person doing the thing. Put them in front; remaining content stretches along the action.

## Let a Sentence Grow Along the Action

Prose often first says what a person did, then continues along the person, thing, or result that appeared. When references are clear, there is no need to repeat the subject in every sentence or add connectors to every clause.

> He pulled a receipt from a drawer, the paper already yellowed. The corner still had a fingerprint, from when mother pressed it back then.

First sentence puts "receipt" at the end; later smoothly catches it. Sentence therefore moves forward without writing first: "that yellowed receipt with the fingerprint mother pressed years ago."

Use this momentum frequently.

- One action brings new things; next sentence can tell about this thing.
- One result appears; next sentence can tell who got affected.
- One judgment lands; next sentence can add its most important basis or exception.
- One person does several things consecutively; when pointing is clear can omit repeated "he."

When two or three people appear in one sentence, omitting creates confusion; write names or titles back. Natural doesn't mean vague.

Long modifiers make readers wait before knowing who or what is being discussed. When modifiers already contain actions, reasons, or time, bring the noun forward and break the history and limits into shorter sentences. Do not pile up nested modifiers before the main subject.

## Knowledge Arrives When Needed

History, technology, systems and numbers all work. First ask why this section needs them.

A background that explains why characters could only choose this way, changes risk size, overturns intuition or makes later results comprehensible—include it. Only proving the author knows a lot—delete it.

Long pieces can detour. Return carrying one thing: could be evidence, a scale, a counter-example, or the author finally admitting earlier thinking was wrong. Detour a loop; original judgment unchanged—probably just knowledge exhibition.

Don't dump all background at once. When readers ask about money, calculate money. When character hits a rule, explain the rule. Knowledge appears where it's needed; readers remember it solved what problem.

## Stop When Readers Can Catch It

When action, objects, dialogue and before-after change already conveys emotion, don't immediately add explanation.

> Old Zhou divided a week's pills into seven compartments. At the door, he came back and opened Wednesday's compartment to look again.

There's already worry and careful concern. Adding "He was very worried about mother getting pills mixed up" will scatter the force.

Same with metaphor. After metaphor lands, don't immediately translate its meaning. When readers can walk the last two or three steps themselves, leave them to the reader. Blank space only spares what readers can already get; key facts, character choice and causality still need clear saying.

## Vernacular Base, Ancient by Feel

Natural vernacular is the foundation. First choose words the speaker would use, readers instantly understand.

"Buy" or "purchase," "fix" or "repair," "see" or "observe," "use" or "employ"—similar meaning, different range. Single syllables lean colloquial; double syllables lean formal. Choose per speaker, topic and context; don't wholesale convert to formal tone; also don't force double syllables to single characters for antiquity.

"Dawn is coming; he still has not come home" is already good prose. "Dawn nears; he comes not" is tighter and gives the tone some distance. Use it only when the surrounding text can support that distance. Product experiences, procedures, and casual chat that suddenly become elevated usually break immersion.

Idioms and allusions stay only when three conditions all hold: meaning's accurate, ordinary readers won't need to look it up, and it's more efficient than vernacular. Allusions needing right-after explanation—delete. Consecutive four-character pacing also loses vernacular breathing room.

Modern vernacular doesn't check tone marks by poem rules, also shouldn't force four-character units for so-called rhythm. Prose first follows how meaning develops, where action happens, pauses land. When meaning flows, sound has origin.

Ancient quality can also come from call-backs and closure. Earlier repeatedly mentions rain; at ending just say "rain hasn't stopped"—already enough. Don't temporarily summon an ancient poem to stamp seals on the whole text.

## Let Sentences Have Give and Take

Real people don't finish every paragraph to identical length. Complex places get two more sentences; already clear places move fast. Sometimes earlier phrasing isn't exact enough, later adds more.

Keep this variation.

- Most paragraphs run two or three sentences, or one paragraph runs long.
- Single-sentence paragraphs need actual pause value, not standing as slogans in a line.
- Same keyword can repeat. Frequently swapping synonyms to dodge repetition makes prose slippery.
- "But," "actually," "later," "also," "so" these common words can stay when truly marking turns, time or tone. Delete only signposts appearing in fixed places, removing doesn't affect meaning.
- Allow normal "I only found out later," "I haven't completely figured this part out" honest adds. Must have actual information after.
- Questions work. They should solve reader understanding gaps, not create suspense by fixed rhythm.

Commas let same-breath action and explanation continue; periods drop one thing temporarily. When logic still connects, sentences can run longer. When action's complete, judgment's landed, period should come.

Short sentences suit landing; long sentences suit developing. Consecutive short sentences sound like table-tapping; consecutive long sentences leave readers waiting endlessly for a foothold. Read aloud: where do you go back to find the subject, where can't you finish in one breath, where does every paragraph stop at the same spot—all need fixing.

Uniformity itself isn't wrong. When entire piece is uniform, readers hear a machine keeping time.

Model-written prose has several measurable problems. Handle them quantitatively when revising.

Sentences too uniform in length. People-written paragraphs have huge gaps between long and short sentences; ten-character sentences sit with forty-character sentences. Model sentences stay similar length. Check every paragraph; when all similar length, pick one sentence to compress under ten characters, or let one stretch over thirty.

Too many connectors. "Because," "so," "but," "meanwhile," "besides," and "however" appear every two sentences in model output. Small clauses can connect through word order and logic. These two passages say the same thing.

> Because business was bad, he decided to use afternoon learning watch repair; it's also another skill to have.

> Business was bad; he had afternoons free, so started learning watch repair. Another skill never hurts.

The second deletes all connectors; logic connects itself. When revising, delete half the connectors; where it doesn't break, don't add back.

Verbs get compressed to nouns. "Made adjustments to the plan" is model-speak; "changed the plan" is how people talk. "Achieved revenue growth" means people say "earned more." When seeing "to...the...er" with a noun after it, return to direct action.

Same word hidden. People don't fear repetition; "repair watches" can appear four times in one section. Model second time says "this skill," third time says "this ability." Swapping makes prose slick. Repeat when you should.

## Concrete Details Must Work

Good details change understanding.

One price shows risk; one quote shows relationship; one failure shows why the old way won't work; one place name explains why someone could only choose that. 

Decorative details only make scenes look true. Pulling at three, the third cigarette, cold coffee on the table, rain outside the window—without sources, without changing what follows, all out.

Fiction can create details. Details still follow character attention and action, not get arranged like props uniformly.

## Judgments Come With Origins

Forum long-piece charm often comes from authors daring to say "I think this thing probably works this way." These judgments can be sharp or biased. Earlier text should show where it comes from.

Don't first display a dumb answer, then announce you see deeper. Say the fact differences straight out.

Also don't grind judgment into fully-right institutional statement. If materials support seventy percent, say to seventy percent. Admit the last thirty you don't know; usually more believable than five more qualifiers.

## No Personal Experience, Don't Write Standard Answers

When users didn't provide experience and the author has no verifiable personal experience, can work with facts, verifiable common processes and reasoning. Can't pretend you've seen it.

This is where one model-style draft appears. Strong thesis up front, then category reasons into six or seven sections; each paragraph adds a qualifier; ending re-summarizes. Might all be correct; readers still touch nothing.

Try another way.

- Only grab one or two most confident reasons now. Answers don't need to handle the whole question.
- When having reliable process materials, can pick one ordinary role, run their action from start to result. Must be clear this is the common situation; can't pair with fake precise experience, can't use model-thought-up situations as long-piece material.
- After abstract explanation, quickly land on one price quote, one piece, one expense, one failure or one decision.
- With only three abstract materials, can't reword each four ways into twelve sections. Every new section must bring new facts, new character, new action, new distinction or new consequence.
- List important exceptions only if they'd change the conclusion. Don't accommodate every direction just to seem complete.
- Use "this also explains," "worse yet," "one more place," "of course," "so" less for paragraph sequencing. Later section directly catches the object or action from earlier.

Forum's good answers are often incomplete, but transparent in one section. Institutional pieces need to cover everything.

When materials run short, first find real cases, data, product flows and counter-examples. Still finding nothing, shorten. Readers don't believe something more because you said it longer.
